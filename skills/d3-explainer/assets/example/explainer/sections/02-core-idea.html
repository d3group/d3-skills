Skipping candidates buys a benchmark, and the price is that the best may be among them

Start from a sentence: every candidate you skip makes your benchmark better and the chance that the best one is already gone larger. The rule fails in exactly two ways, and the cutoff trades one against the other. The figure shows the trade before the algebra does.

Before you drag: with $n = 20$, does skipping half of the candidates beat skipping a quarter?

No, though only just: 0.359 against 0.366. Both are below the optimum of 0.384 at seven skipped. The curve is flat near its top and decreases steeply only at the extremes.

What to keep The optimum balances two failure probabilities. In the limit, the same number, $1/\e$, is the share to skip and the chance of success. For finite $n$ the success probability lies above $1/\e$ and decreases to it as $n$ grows. For this reason, the three lines of code that enumerate the exact optimum are worth it.
