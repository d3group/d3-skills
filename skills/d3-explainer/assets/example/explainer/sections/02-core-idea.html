Skipping candidates buys a benchmark, and the price is that the best may be among them

Start from a sentence: every candidate you skip makes your benchmark better and the chance that the best one is already gone larger. The rule fails in exactly two ways, and the cutoff trades one against the other. The figure shows the trade before the algebra does.

Before you drag: with $n = 20$, does skipping half of the candidates beat skipping a quarter?

No, though only just: 0.359 against 0.366. Both are below the optimum of 0.384 at seven skipped. The curve is flat near its top and falls off steeply only at the extremes.

The same statement, derived

$$\win{\Pn(r)} \;\longrightarrow\; \skip{x} \int_{\skip{x}}^1 \frac{dt}{t} \;=\; -\skip{x} \ln \skip{x}$$
$$P'(x) = -\ln \skip{x} - 1 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \skip{x^\star} = 1/\e, \qquad \win{P(x^\star)} = 1/\e$$
What to keep The optimum balances two failure probabilities, and the same number, $1/\e$, is both the share to skip and the chance of success in the limit. For finite $n$ the success probability lies above $1/\e$ and falls to it, which is why enumerating the exact optimum is worth the three lines of code. Only the first step uses the random arrival order; without that assumption nothing here holds.
